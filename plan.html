How do you turn one idea into four frames?

1 · Story structure

This page turns your one idea into four frames that each have a job to do.

Pixar plans stories with four moves: set it up, make it worse, turn it, land it. Your four frames are those four moves. Work through the lesson below, then use the table to decide which shape your joke is.

Four shapes, four frames

The story spine — once upon a time… every day… until one day… because of that… until finally — is the top row of this table in Year 8 words. Pick the row that fits your roll. Most people use the first one, and the first one is enough.

Four ways to build a four-frame joke. Frame 4 is the punchline in every one of them.
Shape Frame 1 Frame 2 Frame 3 Frame 4 — the punchline
Setup → escalation → twist → punchline SetupThe situation, the character, the place EscalationThe problem grows TwistThe turn nobody saw — usually where your problem card lands PunchlineThe payoff, or the reaction to it
Rule of three Beat 1The pattern starts Beat 2The same thing again Beat 3The same thing again, or the break begins PayoffThe pattern breaks
Status reversal On topWe see who’s winning ChallengedSomething threatens it SlippingThe status shifts ReversedWhoever was winning is losing
The silent beat SetupThe situation, the character, the place EscalationThe problem grows SilenceNo caption at all. Just the pause PunchlineThe payoff, landing into the quiet

3 · Check your shots

The planner asks for a shot type on every frame. They’re the same three words you sorted on 1 Look, and here they are again so you don’t have to go back for them.

The three shots, one more time

  • Establishing — shows the whole place. You can see where you are. Frame 1 is usually one of these.
  • Mid — shows the thing and where it stands. The workhorse. Most of your frames will be these.
  • Close-up — shows one detail, nothing else. Cutting everything out is how you tell a reader what matters.

Two questions before you send it: have you used at least two different shot types? And does the camera get closer as the problem gets worse? Getting closer is how a story speeds up.

Going further

Which shape is your joke — rule of three, status reversal, or a silent frame before the punchline? Name it in the last box of the planner and say why it suits your roll.

Doing six frames instead of four? Use the four boxes for your four main beats and put the two extra in the stuck box.

Stuck? Try this

  • Can’t remember your brief? It’s on the class board from last lesson, under your first name and table number.
  • Stuck on frame 3? It’s the frame that makes the reader think they know what happens next. They should be wrong.
  • Writing too much? Write what we see, in one line, like a caption. Single words count.
  • Doing the two-frame version? Fill in frame 1 and frame 4 and write two-frame version in the frame 2 box. That’s a finished plan.

What good looks like

Read your four lines back. Tick everything that’s true.

  • My four frames tell one story, in order.
  • Frame 4 is the funny one — the surprise lands last.
  • I’ve used at least two different shot types.
  • Something repeats across my frames, and one thing changes at the end.
  • Each frame has one line I could put under the picture.
  • I can name the licence of every image I’m planning to use.

Anything you didn’t tick is your next job. Nobody ticks all six first go.
