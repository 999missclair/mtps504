Where do ideas come from when you haven’t got one?

1 · Watch (10 min)

You’ve read how a comic page works. Now you need something to make a comic about — and that’s the bit that stops people. So you don’t get a blank page. You get a character, a situation and a problem, dealt two at a time, and you pick one of each. Watch the talk first: it’s ten minutes on why a limit can be the thing that starts you.

Watch: why limits make you more creative (10 min). Artist Phil Hansen lost the ability to draw a straight line. Watch what he did next. Notice the moment he says having everything he needed made him freeze. That’s the idea behind your roll — three prompts give you somewhere to start. Captions are on. Heads up: he talks about injuring his hands.

If you’d rather read it, or the pictures aren’t doing much for you: he starts with a tremor that ruins his pointillist drawings, gives up art, then is told to embrace the shake — and rebuilds a practice out of deliberate restrictions, painting on his own chest, on stacks of paper, with karate chops. The turn is near the end: given unlimited materials and unlimited time, he froze and made nothing.

Hate both? One re-roll. It re-deals all three columns and clears what you’ve picked. After that, you’re working with what you’ve got.

That’s your roll. Now make it work.

Pick one from each column. Tap again to change your mind.

3 · Lock it in

One card from each column, then lock it. Locking doesn’t send it anywhere — it just writes your brief out as one line you can copy. Your brief is your three cards written as one line. It’s what you’re working from.

Your brief

You’ll need this three times: on the class board, in the planner, and in the title of your slide. Copy it once and paste it everywhere.

It comes out as one line, like this: A ceramic teapot · The lights go out · They’ve fallen for the sculpture opposite

4 · Put it on the board

Everyone’s roll goes up. You’ll see everyone else’s, and at least one of them will make you think of something for yours.

  1. Paste your brief.
  2. Put it in the column you think is carrying the joke — the who, the where, or the problem.
  3. Say which card you picked and why, in one line.
  4. Read someone else’s and reply with one “what if…”.

Post as your first name and your table number. No surnames. Sam T4 is perfect.

! Reply to one other roll with a “what if…”

Not “that’s good”. Not “lol”. One idea they could use: what if the lights came back on too early? What if the tour group was in every frame?

? Stuck? Try this

Three that already work, if you want to see how someone else got there:

  • A portrait subject, tired of posing · being photographed for the catalogue · they can only be seen from one angle — the photographer keeps moving. They keep turning.
  • A suit of armour · being packed into a crate · someone has touched them — the whole story is one very slow reaction.
  • A dinosaur skeleton · a famous artwork arrives on loan · a crack has appeared, and it’s spreading — it gets worse in every frame. That’s the easiest kind of joke to build.

Going further

Add one of your own: pick an object that has to appear in all four frames. Now the repeat is built in and frame 4 has something to break.

What good looks like

Read your four lines back. Tick everything that’s true.

  • My four frames tell one story, in order.
  • Frame 4 is the funny one — the surprise lands last.
  • I’ve used at least two different shot types.
  • Something repeats across my frames, and one thing changes at the end.
  • Each frame has one line I could put under the picture.
  • I can name the licence of every image I’m planning to use.

Anything you didn’t tick is your next job. Nobody ticks all six first go.
