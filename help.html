Help Centre

Everything on this page is a fix, not a lecture.

Stuck? Start here.

This page covers the four things that go wrong most: working the Comic Builder, finding images you’re allowed to use, writing the credit line, and fixing it when something breaks. Pick a topic below. Every fix is three steps or fewer. Then go back to what you were doing.

Comic Builder basics

Everything you need to do in the builder, in order.

Unlock it with the class password

  1. Go to 4 Build (or 3 Plan) and open the Comic Builder. It opens on a password box.
  2. Type the class password your teacher gave you and press Unlock the builder.
  3. The tool appears. Your rolled story from 2 Roll sits pinned at the top — that’s what goes across the four frames.

No password? Ask your teacher. If you are just looking, use guest mode to add your own pictures and try practice bubbles. Remove bubbles before your final download. GIF search stays in class.

Find a picture — search a GIF

  1. In 1 · Find a picture, type a feeling or a thing — surprise, slow clap, cat — and press Search.
  2. Pick Animated or Still with the toggle. Animated drops in the moving GIF; Still drops in one frozen frame.
  3. Tap a result. It drops straight into the frame that’s active (the one lit up yellow).

GIFs are for planning in class only — they don’t go on the public class wall. That’s the same rule you sorted on 4 Build.

Add your own picture instead

  • Add a picture: press Add a picture and choose a file from the computer. It drops into the active frame.
  • Paste: copy an image anywhere, then just paste (Ctrl+V, or Cmd+V on a Mac) with the builder open. It lands in the active frame.

Make sure the frame you want is active first — click it so it lights up yellow.

Use captions, not dialogue

  1. The final story has no character dialogue. Do not leave speech bubbles in the comic you download.
  2. Press Add caption for a short line that explains what a panel shows. It lands in the active frame ready to type.
  3. A caption is how someone who can’t see the picture still gets the joke, and it is part of what is marked.

Arrange the four frames

It’s a 2×2 comic. Read across: set-up · build-up · build-up · punchline.

  • Choose a frame: click it so it lights up yellow. Everything you add lands there.
  • Move: drag any picture or bubble where you want it.
  • Resize: drag the little corner handle.
  • Delete: tap the × on the item.

Without the mouse: click an item, then arrow keys move it, Shift + arrow keys resize it, and Delete removes it.

Download your finished comic

  1. When all four frames are done, press Download my comic.
  2. It saves the whole 2×2 comic as a PNG to the laptop’s Downloads folder.
  3. Post it to the class wall in the same lesson.

The builder only saves on this device. Nothing is in the cloud, and the laptop gets wiped — so download before you leave. Need to start over? Clear all resets every frame.

Finding images

Start in the class image bank. It’s faster, and everything in it is already checked.

The class image bank — start here

The class image bank → holds pictures already matched to the roll, already licence-checked, with the credit line already written out. Find your card, pick a picture, copy the credit line, done. No licence to read, no search to run.

Use the open web (below) only if the bank doesn’t have what you want, or you’re doing the extension.

Three places to search the open web, and what to do when nothing comes up.

Openverse — start here

Openverse searches millions of openly licensed images at once, and it shows you the licence next to the picture. That is why it is first.

  1. Go to openverse.org and type what you want.
  2. On the results page, open the filters and tick a licence — CC0 and CC BY are the easiest to use.
  3. Click a result. The licence is written on that image’s page, under or beside the picture.
  4. Press the download button on that page.

Keep the tab open. You need what’s on it for your credit line.

Wikimedia Commons — huge, but read every file

Commons has everything, including whole museum collections. The catch is that the licence changes from file to file. There is no single Commons licence.

  1. Go to commons.wikimedia.org and search.
  2. Click a result to open its file page — the page with the big picture and a wall of information under it.
  3. Scroll to the Licensing section. It is a coloured box near the bottom. It will say Public domain, or CC BY-SA 3.0, or something similar.
  4. Click Original file (or Download) to save it.

If you can’t find a Licensing box, don’t use that file.

The Met — everything you can take is CC0

The Metropolitan Museum of Art has released about half a million artworks with no restrictions at all. Paintings, prints, sculpture, armour, teapots.

  1. Go to the Met’s collection search and type what you want.
  2. Turn on the Open Access filter. Now every result is free to use.
  3. Open an artwork. Look for Open Access and Public domain on the page.
  4. Download the image from that page.

If a result does not say Open Access, the Met has not released it. Search again.

Searching well — and what to do when nothing comes up

  • Use concrete nouns. Teapot, not something lonely. If you can’t photograph it, you can’t search for it.
  • Two words, not six. Empty bus stop beats a sad empty bus stop at night in the rain.
  • Search the place or the object, not the character. Can’t find a retired greyhound losing its lead? Try empty park, or a close-up of one object, and let the caption do the rest.
  • Change the word. Bin → rubbish bin → street. Different words, different pictures.

Getting the file into your comic

  1. Download the image. It goes into the laptop’s Downloads folder.
  2. Go back to the Comic Builder, click the frame you want, then press Add a picture and choose the file — or just paste it in.
  3. Then go and find the next one.

Do them one at a time. The Downloads folder belongs to the laptop, not to you, and you probably won’t get the same laptop next lesson. And the builder only saves on this device — so once your comic is together, Download my comic before you leave. Four images sitting in Downloads at the end of the lesson are four images you have lost.

Crediting images

How to read a licence, and how to turn it into your credit line.

The line you are writing

Four frames, four credit lines. Each one has the same four parts, in the same order:

Title — Creator — Source — Licence

You already know the rule from the Build page: anything you post publicly uses an image whose licence you can name. This page is how you find out what to name.

Two questions, not one

In class, for the joke, you can use it. Posting publicly, you need a licence you can name.

Not sure about a particular picture? Ask your teacher.

If your image came from the class image bank

You don’t need any of this. Copy the finished credit line straight off the class image bank → card. The rest of this page is for images you found yourself.

Openverse — where the licence is, and a real example

The licence sits on the image’s own page in Openverse, written out in words, with a link to what it means.

  1. Open the image’s page in Openverse.
  2. Read the title and the creator at the top.
  3. Read the licence — it will be CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA or CC BY-NC.
  4. Write the four parts down before you close the tab.

Worked example. Searching teapot on Openverse returns a photograph called Teapot reflection, taken by Dai Lygad, licensed CC BY 2.0. The line is:

Teapot reflection — Dai Lygad — Openverse — CC BY 2.0

Wikimedia Commons — where the licence is, and a real example

Every Commons file has a Licensing box on its file page. That box, not the search results, is where the licence lives.

  1. Open the file page.
  2. Take the title from the top of the page.
  3. Take the creator from the Author row in the information table.
  4. Take the licence from the Licensing box further down.

Same four-part format as above. Watch this one — CC BY-SA is share-alike — see below.

The Met — where the licence is, and a real example

The Met makes this easy. If an artwork is Open Access, it is CC0, and the artwork’s page says so.

  1. Open the artwork’s page.
  2. Take the title and the artist from the top.
  3. Check the page says Open Access / Public domain.
  4. The licence is CC0.

Same four-part format as above. Full title example on 4 Build →

What the licences actually mean

LicenceWhat it means for you
CC0 The maker gave up their rights. You can use it however you like. Credit them anyway — it’s good manners and it’s part of this task
CC BY Use it for anything, as long as you name the maker
CC BY-SA Use it and name the maker — but anything you build from it has to carry the same licence. Your whole comic gets that licence too
CC BY-NC Name the maker, and don’t make money from it. A class wall is fine. Selling T-shirts is not
Public domain Old enough, or released freely enough, that nobody owns it any more. Use it. Still name the artist

No licence stated means you can’t post it. Not “probably fine”. Not “it was on Google Images”. If the page doesn’t tell you the licence, you don’t know what you’re allowed to do with it, and a public board is the wrong place to find out. In your own comic, in class, it is a different question — see Two questions, not one above. The three sources on this page exist so that the easy answer is also the safe one.

Where your credit lines go

Two places, and you need both:

  1. The planning form, one attribution line per frame, as you find each image. Do it then, not later — you will not remember.
  2. Your comic, in a caption along the bottom. Add a caption in the builder and type the four lines, one per frame. They have to be readable in the finished PNG.

When it breaks

Find your problem. Do the three steps. Get back to work.

“The tool asks for a class password.”
  1. That’s meant to happen — the builder is for class use only.
  2. Type the password your teacher gave you and press Unlock the builder.
  3. Haven’t got it, or it won’t work? Ask your teacher. Nobody else can let you in.
“My picture won’t add.”
  1. Tap a frame first so it lights up yellow — pictures land in the active frame, so if none is chosen there’s nowhere for it to go.
  2. From a search? Try a different word. From your own file? Use Add a picture, or copy the image and paste it in.
  3. Still nothing? The picture may not be an image file. Find another one.
“The picture came in tiny.” / “The picture came in huge and covered everything.”
  1. Click it once so the handles appear.
  2. Drag the corner handle until it fits the frame.
  3. No mouse? Click it, then hold Shift and press the arrow keys to resize.
“I can’t move or delete something.”
  1. Click the item once so its handles show.
  2. Drag it to move, drag the corner to resize, tap the × to delete.
  3. No mouse? Arrow keys move it, Delete removes it.
“The download didn’t work.”
  1. A picture in the comic blocked the save. It happens with some GIFs.
  2. Take a screenshot of your comic instead — on a Mac, press Shift-Cmd-4 and drag a box around it.
  3. Post the screenshot to the class wall the same way.
“Nothing I make is saved.”
  1. The builder only saves on this device — there’s no cloud and no account.
  2. So Download my comic before you leave, every lesson.
  3. The laptop gets wiped and you may not get the same one next time. The PNG in your Downloads (and on the class wall) are what survive.
“My rolled story isn’t showing at the top.”
  1. You may have come straight to the builder without rolling.
  2. Go back to 2 Roll and roll your story — it’ll appear pinned at the top.
  3. In a hurry? Type or paste it into the story box at the top of the builder.
“The website / the Padlet won’t load.”
  1. Tell the teacher. It’s probably the school’s filter, not you.
  2. Try the other image source in the meantime — if one is blocked, the others usually aren’t.
  3. Nothing you’ve made in the builder is lost — it’s saved on this device. Just remember to download it before you leave.
“The image source is blocked.”
  1. Use one of the other two. The task doesn’t change.
  2. The Met and Wikimedia Commons are museum and encyclopedia sites and are very rarely blocked.
  3. Tell the teacher which one was blocked so it can be sorted for next lesson.
“The Padlet won’t take my post.”
  1. It may be asking for a nickname. Use your fictional character name and table code. Never use the name of a student, teacher, friend or real person.
  2. File too big? Post the comic PNG on its own, nothing else.
  3. Still stuck, and the post is written? Screenshot it so you don’t lose the words, then ask the teacher.
“I don’t want a name on the class wall.”
  1. That’s fine — hand the PNG to me instead, by email or in Classroom.
  2. I’ll post it for you using only your table code.
  3. It counts exactly the same as posting it yourself — same comments, same marking.
“The file I saved won’t add to my comic.”
  1. Check what you actually saved. Right-clicking a picture sometimes saves the web page instead of the image.
  2. Go back to the source and use its Download button rather than right-clicking, then Add a picture again.
  3. The builder takes normal image files (JPG, PNG, GIF). If it’s something else, find a different image.
“I can’t find the licence anywhere on the page.”
  1. On Commons, scroll down to the Licensing box. On Openverse it’s beside the picture. On the Met it says Open Access.
  2. Genuinely not there? Then you can’t use that image.
  3. Go back and search again. There are millions of others.

Still stuck

Ask the person next to you first — half of this list is something someone at your table fixed ten minutes ago. Then ask the teacher.

This website can’t see your work. It doesn’t know your name, it doesn’t store your comic, and it can’t get anything back for you. Your comic is saved on the device you built it on, and your finished strip is on the class Padlet, and those are the two places to look.

  • No account, no cookies. Your story and comic stay on this device.
  • Your teacher is in the room, and quicker than this page.
