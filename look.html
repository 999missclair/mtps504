Pro Artist’s Guide to Comic & Manga Layouts, Paneling, Flow

Steve Ellis · Art Rocket · about 8 minutes to read

Read: how comic artists build a page. Comic artist Steve Ellis explains the tools every cartoonist uses. Read the sections on panels, gutters and shot types. As you read, notice two things: how the space between panels changes how fast the story feels, and how a close-up and a wide shot do completely different jobs. Heads up — it’s an American site, so the spelling looks different.

The diagrams on that page don’t come with descriptions, so here is what they show: the same page laid out twice, once with wide gaps between the panels and once with narrow ones; and the same moment drawn three times, as a wide shot, a mid shot and a close-up.

2 · Panel Scramble

Six panels from one story. Sort them by how close the camera is. Tap a panel, then tap the bin you think it belongs in — and if it’s wrong, it comes straight back with a clue.

There’s no real camera, but every panel is drawn as if a camera stood somewhere. “Shot” means where it’s standing.

        0 of 6 placed.

        Keyboard: press Enter or Space to pick a panel up, Tab to a bin, then Enter or Space to put it down. Escape puts it back.

        What do these shots do?

        You’ve sorted them. Now say what they do. There’s no wrong answer here — just say what’s true for you.

        Establishing. You’ve just been shown the whole room. How does it feel?

        Mid. Now you can see the thing and where it stands. What changed?

        Close-up. Now you can only see one detail. What does that do?

        Going further

        These six panels are one story.

        1. Put them in an order that works. You can only use four. Which two do you cut, and what does the reader have to work out on their own?

        Stuck? Try this

        • Can’t place a panel? Ask one question: can I tell what room I’m in? If yes, it’s establishing. If you can only see one thing, it’s a close-up.
        • Three bins feel like too many? Press Try it with two bins at the top of the board. Same panels, one easier question.
        • Not sure what the words mean? The definitions stay under the bin names the whole time. You’re not meant to remember them.

        What good looks like

        Read your four lines back. Tick everything that’s true.

        • My four frames tell one story, in order.
        • Frame 4 is the funny one — the surprise lands last.
        • I’ve used at least two different shot types.
        • Something repeats across my frames, and one thing changes at the end.
        • Each frame has one line I could put under the picture.
        • I can name the licence of every image I’m planning to use.

        Anything you didn’t tick is your next job. Nobody ticks all six first go.
